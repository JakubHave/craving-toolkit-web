# Craving Toolkit
## Practical tools to fight urges, stop spirals, and stay in recovery

**Version 1 draft**

---

## Important note
This guide is educational and based on lived experience, recovery reading, and practical treatment frameworks. It is **not** medical advice and is **not** a substitute for professional treatment, therapy, detox, or emergency support.

If you are in immediate danger, at risk of overdose, severely withdrawing, or afraid you may harm yourself or someone else, seek urgent professional help right now.

---

## Introduction
If you are reading this, you probably already know something painful:

A craving can feel bigger than logic.

In the calm light of day, you may know exactly what alcohol, drugs, binge eating, or another compulsive behavior has cost you. You may know what happens next. You may swear to yourself that this time will be different.

And then the moment comes.

Stress hits. Shame hits. Loneliness hits. Boredom hits. You see a trigger, remember a feeling, or just suddenly feel pulled. Your mind starts bargaining. Your body gets restless. Part of you already feels halfway gone.

This guide is for that moment.

Not for theory. Not for impressing anyone. Not for pretending recovery is neat.

For the hard window between **craving** and **action**.

I’m writing this from lived experience, not from a distance. I have my own history with addiction and compulsive behavior, including overeating, alcohol, and drugs. I’ve also spent years learning from treatment, rehab, recovery work, and the writing of people who have thought deeply about addiction from different angles: trauma, neuroscience, habits, motivation, community, and behavior change.

The good news is this:

A craving is powerful, but it is not permanent.  
A craving is intense, but it is not a command.  
A slip is serious, but it does not have to become a collapse.

This guide will help you:
- understand what cravings are
- survive the first few minutes without making things worse
- break the spiral before it becomes a relapse or binge
- handle different triggers more intelligently
- build your own anti-craving plan
- recover faster and with more honesty if you slip

You do not need perfect motivation to use this guide.

You just need enough honesty to admit: **I need tools for the moment when my brain stops feeling trustworthy.**

## 1. What a craving really is
A craving is not just “wanting something.”

A craving is usually a whole-state experience. It can include:
- body tension
- agitation
- mental obsession
- emotional discomfort
- tunnel vision
- fantasy about relief
- bargaining and rationalization
- urgency

It often feels like the addictive behavior is the answer.

That is the trap.

### A craving is usually a wave
Many practical recovery approaches teach some version of this: cravings rise, peak, and pass.

That does **not** mean they are pleasant. It means they are temporary.

The problem is that when a craving is high, it lies to you.
It says:
- this will not pass
- you need relief now
- just once is fine
- you can start over tomorrow
- you’ve already failed anyway

A craving often feels permanent because your nervous system is activated. But the feeling is not permanent. What makes it worse is feeding it with attention, fantasy, access, and secrecy.

### Craving is not only about pleasure
A lot of addictive behavior is less about joy than people think.

Sometimes the craving is for:
- numbness
- relief
- quiet
- escape
- reward
- comfort
- self-erasure
- ritual
- familiarity

That matters.

If you only ask, “Why do I want this?” you may get a shallow answer.
A better question is:

**What do I believe this will do for me right now?**

Maybe the answer is:
- calm me down
- help me not feel alone
- let me stop thinking
- give me a break
- make me feel something
- let me disappear

That answer tells you more about the craving than shame ever will.

### Addiction is not one simple thing
Different authors and recovery traditions explain addiction differently:
- some focus on trauma and emotional pain
- some focus on learning and neuroplasticity
- some focus on dopamine and reward
- some focus on habit loops
- some focus on spirituality and surrender
- some focus on motivation, environment, and support

You do not need to pick one religion of explanation.

For practical purposes, it is enough to understand this:

**Your brain and body can learn to associate a substance or behavior with relief, reward, escape, or survival. Then the urge can become fast, emotional, and automatic.**

That is why knowledge alone often fails in the heat of the moment.

Which is exactly why you need tools.

## 2. Why cravings feel bigger than logic
When people are not addicted, they often say things like:
- just say no
- think about the consequences
- be strong
- use self-control

That sounds nice. It often fails.

Why?

Because a craving is not a debate club.

In a craving state, several things can happen at once:
- your attention narrows
- your memory becomes selective
- your body wants fast relief
- your mind highlights the reward and hides the cost
- your old ritual starts running automatically

This is why people do things that make no sense from the outside.
It is also why shaming yourself for “being stupid” is not useful.

### The hot brain and the cold brain
When you are calm, you can think clearly. Let’s call that your **cold state**.
When you are triggered, restless, ashamed, or activated, you are in a **hot state**.

Your cold brain says:
- I never want to do that again
- tomorrow matters
- my family matters
- my body matters
- this always ends badly

Your hot brain says:
- just a little
- just today
- I need relief first, then I’ll fix my life
- I already feel terrible, so why not
- I deserve something

Recovery gets stronger when you stop expecting your hot brain to behave like your cold brain.

That is why planning matters.

### You need fewer decisions in the hard moment
If a craving hits and your whole plan is “I’ll make a wise choice,” you are underprepared.

A stronger plan is:
- a written script
- a list of emergency actions
- a person to contact
- physical barriers between you and the substance/behavior
- one or two default moves you always make first

In other words:
**Don’t rely on inspiration. Use structure.**

## 3. The first 10 minutes: emergency tools
This may be the most important chapter in the book.

When a strong urge hits, do **not** try to solve your whole life.
Your job is smaller:

**Get through the next 10 minutes without feeding the craving.**

Below are practical tools. You do not need all of them. Pick the ones that fit you and make them your default response.

### Tool 1: Delay
Tell yourself:

**“I am not deciding forever. I am waiting 10 minutes.”**

This matters because cravings hate delay. They want immediacy.

A delay:
- creates a gap between urge and action
- reduces automaticity
- gives your nervous system time to shift
- lets your wiser self come back online

If 10 minutes feels impossible, start with 3.
But delay on purpose.

### Tool 2: Leave the environment
If the trigger is around you, get out.

Examples:
- leave the bar
- leave the kitchen
- put down the phone
- get out of bed if you are spiraling
- walk out of the shop aisle
- go where using, ordering, binging, or hiding becomes harder

Do not underestimate this.
Sometimes the most spiritual move is simply **leaving the room**.

### Tool 3: Make the body safer
Cravings are often intensified by body state.
Ask:
- am I exhausted?
- am I hungry?
- am I dehydrated?
- am I keyed up and physically flooded?

Fast body tools:
- drink water
- eat something stabilizing if you are underfed
- wash face with cold water or take a cold shower
- go for a fast walk
- do 20 air squats or pushups
- breathe slowly and deeply for 1–2 minutes

This does not solve addiction. It lowers the temperature.
Sometimes that is the difference between relapse and no relapse.

### Tool 4: Say the urge out loud
The craving gets stronger in secrecy.

Try saying:
- “I am craving right now.”
- “My brain is trying to sell me a bad idea.”
- “I am in danger of acting on this.”

Simple. Honest. Not dramatic.

If possible, send it to someone:
- “I’m having a strong urge. I need 10 minutes of contact.”
- “I’m not okay right now and I need accountability.”

### Tool 5: Read a recovery card
Write this on your phone or on paper before you need it.

Example:

**When I crave, my brain lies.**  
This feeling will pass.  
If I use / binge / act out, I will get short relief and longer pain.  
My job is to survive this wave.  
Next steps: leave, water, walk, message someone, wait 10 minutes.

Do not improvise wisdom while triggered. Pre-write it.

### Tool 6: Make access harder
If you have any chance to increase friction, do it.

Examples:
- hand over cards/cash
- block apps/sites
- delete delivery apps
- avoid carrying substances
- don’t keep binge food in the house if possible
- break the route to access
- go where you cannot use privately

A lot of relapse prevention is not philosophical. It is logistical.

### Tool 7: Substitute action, not just thought
Do something that competes with the craving state.

Examples:
- walk fast
- clean something
- go where people are
- listen to a recovery talk
- call a person who knows the truth
- eat a proper meal if hunger is part of the spiral
- write the consequence chain

The goal is not fake positivity.
The goal is interruption.

## 4. Urge surfing: how to ride out a craving without obeying it
One of the most useful ideas in modern craving work is this:

**You do not have to fight every craving head-on. You can ride it.**

Urge surfing means you treat the craving like a wave instead of a command.

### How to do it
1. **Pause.** Do not move automatically.
2. **Name it.** “This is a craving.”
3. **Locate it in the body.** Where is it? Chest? Throat? Stomach? Jaw? Hands?
4. **Describe it.** Tight? Hot? Buzzing? Hollow? Pulling? Heavy?
5. **Breathe and watch.** Let it rise without immediately acting.
6. **Get curious.** What is this actually like, right now, in this second?
7. **Notice change.** Even intense sensations shift if you observe them.

### Why this works
A craving often wins because it fuses with your identity.
It becomes:
- I need this
- I am this
- I can’t stand this

Urge surfing changes the frame.
Now it becomes:
- I am noticing a strong wave of wanting
- it has body sensations
- it is changing moment by moment
- I can observe it without obeying it

This may sound small. It is not.
It is the difference between drowning in the wave and learning to stay on top of it.

### A short urge-surfing script
Try this:

> I’m having a craving.  
> I don’t need to solve it right this second.  
> I’m going to notice what it feels like in my body.  
> It may rise, peak, and fall.  
> I can ride this wave for the next two minutes.

Start with two minutes if that is all you can do.
Then continue.

## 5. The addictive voice: how your mind talks you into it
Many relapses start with a sentence.

Not always a loud one. Often a quiet one.

Something like:
- just once
- this doesn’t count
- I’ll start again tomorrow
- I’ve earned it
- nobody will know
- I already messed up anyway
- it’s not even that serious
- I need relief first

This is the addictive voice.

Call it what you want:
- the salesman
- the trickster
- the addict brain
- the lie machine
- the bargain voice

The name matters less than recognizing the pattern.

### The addictive voice usually does three things
1. **Minimizes the cost**  
   “It won’t be that bad.”

2. **Magnifies the reward**  
   “This will finally make me feel okay.”

3. **Erases the future**  
   “Just deal with now.”

### How to answer it
You do not need a perfect speech. You need a practiced one.

Examples:
- “That’s the urge talking, not wisdom.”
- “Short relief, long pain.”
- “If I start, stopping gets harder.”
- “I know where this goes.”
- “The first hit / drink / binge is not the whole story.”
- “I am craving relief, not actually wanting the consequences.”

### Write your top five lies
Do this exercise now, not later.

Write:
1. The lie my addiction tells me most often
2. What usually happens if I believe it
3. The truth I need instead

Example:
- Lie: “One drink will calm me down.”
- Reality: “It lowers my guard, then I want more, then tomorrow is wrecked.”
- Truth: “What I need is regulation, not a first drink.”

This is powerful because it converts vague danger into recognizable script.

## 6. Know your triggers
Cravings rarely appear out of nowhere.

There is usually a setup.

### Common triggers
- stress
- loneliness
- resentment
- shame
- boredom
- celebration / reward mode
- exhaustion
- conflict
- hunger
- certain people
- certain places
- certain times of day
- money in hand
- being alone with access
- social media / digital spirals
- old music, neighborhoods, routines, or rituals

### Trigger awareness is not weakness
Some people think that if they were stronger, they wouldn’t need to think about triggers.
That is nonsense.

A diabetic who watches sugar is not weak. A person with a bad back who watches lifting form is not weak. A person recovering from addiction who watches triggers is not weak.

It is intelligent.

### Use the chain, not just the event
Instead of asking, “Why did I do it?” ask:

What was the chain?

Example:
- slept badly
- skipped breakfast
- stressful meeting
- felt angry and unseen
- saw opportunity to numb out
- told myself I deserved relief
- isolated
- acted

If you only focus on the final act, you miss the entire runway.

### Trigger map exercise
Write down:
- my top 5 triggers
- my top 5 danger times
- my top 5 danger places
- the first signs I’m getting pulled in
- what helps most when I catch it early

This becomes your personal prevention map.

## 7. Different triggers need different tools
Not all cravings are the same.
Trying to use one tool for every trigger is like using the same key for every lock.

### Stress and overwhelm
When the trigger is stress, the craving often says:
“I need relief now.”

Best tools:
- breathe and slow down your body
- leave the pressure environment if possible
- write the next 3 non-destructive steps only
- ask for help sooner
- reduce input: noise, screens, chaos
- do one grounding action before any decision

### Loneliness and emptiness
When the trigger is loneliness, the addictive pull may feel like:
“I need connection or I need to disappear.”

Best tools:
- contact a real person
- go where people are, even if you don’t want to talk much
- avoid isolating with full access
- do not trust loneliness to tell you the truth about your worth

### Boredom and restlessness
This one is underrated.
A lot of addictive acting-out is not drama. It is deadness.

Best tools:
- movement
- structured activity
- change of environment
- something slightly effortful but concrete
- preplanned “boredom alternatives” list

### Shame
Shame is one of the most dangerous triggers because it says:
“You’re already disgusting, so why even try?”

Best tools:
- tell the truth fast
- refuse self-abuse language
- do one repair action immediately
- remember: shame pushes you toward the very thing that creates more shame

### Celebration / reward mode
A lot of people relapse when things are going well.
The story becomes:
“I’ve earned this.”

Best tools:
- name the trigger clearly
- plan sober rewards in advance
- remember that “reward” is often just a trap in a nice outfit

## 8. Different addiction patterns
There are shared principles across addictions, but some patterns matter.

### Alcohol
Challenges:
- socially normalized
- often tied to reward, relaxation, or belonging
- the “just one” lie is common

Helpful focus:
- first-drink prevention
- social planning
- exit plans for events
- replacing the evening ritual

### Drugs
Challenges:
- fast escalation
- higher medical risk
- strong cue conditioning
- certain contacts / places can be highly dangerous

Helpful focus:
- hard boundaries around people and access
- rapid honesty when craving starts
- not staying alone with means or opportunity
- urgent outside support when needed

### Overeating / binge eating patterns
Challenges:
- food cannot be fully avoided
- emotional numbing and self-soothing can be deeply tied to food
- restriction can intensify rebound behavior

Helpful focus:
- body stabilization
- reducing extremes of hunger
- understanding emotional triggers
- interrupting secrecy and “I’ll restart tomorrow” loops
- compassionate consistency, not punishment

### Process / behavioral addictions
Examples might include gambling, porn, shopping, gaming, scrolling, or other compulsive behaviors.

Challenges:
- constant digital access
- no physical substance to throw away
- denial can be easier
- rituals are often invisible to others

Helpful focus:
- blockers and friction
- changing private routines
- device/environment redesign
- honesty and accountability

## 9. Different personality styles in addiction
This chapter is not about boxing people into labels. It is about pattern recognition.

### The all-or-nothing person
Typical thoughts:
- perfect or useless
- one mistake ruins everything
- if I slip, I may as well go all the way

Helpful tools:
- “one mistake is not the whole week” reminder
- small reset actions
- anti-catastrophe language
- progress over purity

### The secretive isolator
Typical pattern:
- keeps everything hidden
- looks okay externally
- spirals in private

Helpful tools:
- forced honesty systems
- regular check-ins
- do not let craving stay unspoken
- reduce private access

### The rebel / self-saboteur
Typical thoughts:
- don’t tell me what to do
- I want relief on my own terms
- I hate feeling controlled

Helpful tools:
- frame recovery as self-respect, not obedience
- choose your own rules consciously
- notice when rebellion is actually self-harm wearing a cool jacket

### The high-performer
Typical pattern:
- appears successful
- uses achievement to hide pain
- crashes in private

Helpful tools:
- build non-performance-based support
- admit emotional reality sooner
- do not let success become a permission slip

### The comfort-seeker
Typical pattern:
- reaches for soothing quickly
- avoids discomfort at almost any cost

Helpful tools:
- distress tolerance
- body regulation
- short discomfort training
- remembering that soothing and healing are not always the same thing

## 10. Shame, slips, and the danger of “screw it”
One of the most destructive patterns in addiction is not the first mistake.
It is what comes after the first mistake.

The pattern looks like this:
- I slipped
- I feel ashamed
- I attack myself
- I don’t want to feel this
- I use / binge / act out more
- now I feel worse
- now I really can’t tell the truth

This is the **collapse spiral**.

### A slip is serious, but it is not a command to continue
The old mentality says:
- I already failed
- today is ruined
- I’ll restart Monday

That mindset has destroyed a lot of recoveries.

A better mindset is:
- I slipped
- I need honesty and interruption immediately
- the next hour matters more than my self-hatred

### What to do after a slip
1. **Stop the sequence fast**  
   Don’t turn one event into a weekend.

2. **Tell the truth quickly**  
   To yourself, and ideally to someone else.

3. **Remove access**  
   Do not stay in the middle of the setup.

4. **Do not perform a shame ritual**  
   No long speeches about being trash. No self-destruction theater.

5. **Review the chain later, not while spiraling**  
   Ask what happened without brutality.

6. **Reset in the next hour**  
   Not next week. Not next month.

### What not to do after a slip
- disappear
- binge harder because “the streak is broken”
- make wild promises instead of concrete action
- attack yourself until you want relief again

Recovery grows through fast honesty, not dramatic self-hatred.

## 11. Daily practices that make cravings weaker over time
Recovery is not only what you do during the craving.
It is also what you build around your life so cravings have less power.

### Sleep
Exhaustion makes everything harder.
If you are sleep-deprived, you are more impulsive, less regulated, and more likely to seek quick relief.

### Food stability
This matters especially for overeating, alcohol recovery, and people who get destabilized when underfed.
Extreme hunger can make a craving much stronger.

### Movement
You do not need to become a fitness influencer.
But your nervous system benefits from movement.
Walking alone helps more than many people admit.

### Routine
Chaos is expensive.
A simple routine reduces decision fatigue and lowers the number of danger windows.

### Less secrecy
Secrets feed addiction.
A recovery-supporting life contains more truth.
Not total exposure to everyone. Just less hiding.

### Fewer triggers within reach
Do not make a high-risk life your default environment.
Recovery often requires boring, practical changes.
That is not weakness. That is wisdom.

### Meaning and identity
If your whole life is just “not doing the bad thing,” recovery gets thin.
You need something you are building:
- relationships
- health
- work
- service
- creativity
- faith
- peace
- dignity

You do not beat addiction only by removing something. You also weaken it by building a life that makes returning less attractive.

## 12. Build your personal anti-craving plan
Do this in writing.
A plan in your head is easy to negotiate with.
A written plan is harder to lie around.

### Part 1: My warning signs
What tells you you’re entering a danger zone?
Examples:
- skipping meals
- isolating
- fantasizing
- hiding purchases
- scrolling certain content
- telling yourself “I deserve it”
- replaying old using / binge memories

### Part 2: My top triggers
List them clearly.

### Part 3: My first 3 emergency actions
Example:
1. Leave the environment
2. Text one person
3. Walk for 10 minutes with no phone access to the trigger

### Part 4: My anti-lie statements
Write 3–5 sentences you need when your mind starts bargaining.

### Part 5: My support people
Who can you contact when you are at risk?
Put names and numbers somewhere easy.

### Part 6: What I must reduce access to
Be specific.
- cards
- cash
- apps
- contacts
- locations
- certain foods
- certain routes home
- being alone in certain states

### Part 7: My reason to stay clean / steady
Do not write a fake inspirational speech.
Write something true.

Examples:
- I want peace more than chaos.
- I want honesty.
- I want my body back.
- I want to stop disappearing from my own life.
- I want to respect myself.

## 14. When you need more than a guide
This guide can help. It may even help a lot.
But sometimes a guide is not enough.

You may need outside help if:
- cravings feel unmanageable most days
- stopping causes dangerous withdrawal
- you are hiding severe use or binge behavior
- you repeatedly relapse alone
- you feel suicidal, hopeless, or out of control
- your addiction is creating serious medical, legal, or relationship danger

Support can include:
- therapy
- addiction counseling
- rehab / treatment
- peer support groups
- recovery coaching
- medication-assisted treatment when appropriate
- honest family or friend support

Needing support is not failure.
Trying to white-knuckle everything forever often is.

## 15. Final words
If addiction has been part of your life, you already know this is not a simple battle between good intentions and bad behavior.

It is deeper than that.
It gets into your body, your rituals, your self-talk, your pain, your loneliness, your reward system, your identity.

But this is also true:
You are not only a craving machine.
You are not only your worst pattern.
You are not only what you did in the darkest moment.

Recovery is rarely clean and cinematic.
It is often repetitive, humble, honest, and stubborn.

Sometimes it looks like:
- leaving the room
- telling the truth sooner
- eating before you spiral
- deleting the app
- calling someone before the binge, not after
- refusing the first compromise
- surviving one ugly wave without obeying it

That counts.
That matters.
That is how people get better.

Not always in one big breakthrough.
Often in many smaller acts of truth.

If you’re in a hard moment right now, keep it simple:

Drink water.  
Leave the trigger.  
Tell the truth.  
Wait 10 minutes.  
Do the next sane thing.  
Then do another one.

That is enough for now.
